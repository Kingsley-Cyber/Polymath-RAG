# Lab Notes: Caffeine, Alertness, and Task Switching

These notes review the evidence on caffeine and its interaction with alertness and task switching. The consistent pattern across controlled studies is that a moderate dose of caffeine reduces subjective sleepiness and improves performance on simple vigilance tasks, while the effects on more complex executive tasks are smaller and less reliable. Part of the disagreement between studies reflects differences in habitual use: regular consumers show smaller responses than occasional consumers, which complicates any single dose-response curve.

Task switching is measured with paradigms that alternate between two simple rules, and the performance cost of a switch is usually expressed as the difference in response time between switch trials and repeat trials. Caffeine narrows this cost in some experiments and leaves it unchanged in others. One plausible explanation is that the switch cost has multiple components, and caffeine affects only the components related to alertness. A claim that caffeine improves executive control in general would therefore be an overstatement of the current evidence.

The dose and the timing of measurement matter in specific ways. Blood caffeine concentration peaks roughly thirty to sixty minutes after ingestion, and studies that measure performance too early or too late may miss the effect entirely. Withdrawal is a further confound: participants who are deprived of their usual caffeine perform below their own baseline before receiving the dose, which can make a null dose effect look like an improvement. Designs that control for withdrawal find smaller effects than designs that do not.

Subjective and objective measures diverge in this literature just as they do in sleep research. Participants often report feeling more alert after caffeine even when their performance on the objective task does not change, and the correlation between self-reported alertness and measured vigilance is modest. For this reason the notes treat subjective alertness and behavioral vigilance as separate constructs, and any extraction model should keep them separate as well.

The practical conclusion is mild. Caffeine may support sustained attention in fatigued individuals, and the effect depends on dose, timing, tolerance, and the measure used. Statements that describe caffeine as a cause of improved cognitive performance simplify a literature in which the association is real but conditional, and any downstream summary should preserve that conditionality.
