TITLE: Monitoring Accuracy, Cognitive Load, and the Regulation of Learning

Metacognitive monitoring refers to a learner’s assessment of what they know, how well they understand material, and how likely they are to remember it later. Metacognitive control refers to the decisions made on the basis of those assessments, such as whether to continue studying a difficult concept, revisit an earlier chapter, or stop practicing material judged to be mastered. The two processes interact but are not identical. A learner can monitor performance accurately and still choose an ineffective study strategy, while poor monitoring can produce misguided control decisions even when the available learning strategies are otherwise effective.

Judgments of learning are one common form of metacognitive monitoring. Immediately after reading a familiar explanation, students may assign high confidence because the material feels fluent, but processing fluency does not necessarily predict later retrieval. This familiarity effect can create an illusion of competence: the learner interprets ease of processing as evidence that the information has been learned. Delaying a judgment until after an attempt to retrieve the material often provides a better diagnostic signal because the learner must reconstruct the information rather than merely recognize it.

Working-memory demands can also interfere with monitoring. When a task requires a learner to maintain several intermediate steps, compare competing representations, and suppress irrelevant information, cognitive load consumes resources that might otherwise support error detection. High load does not automatically eliminate metacognition, but monitoring may become less precise when attention is divided between performing the task and evaluating performance. This distinction matters because a learner who fails to notice an error cannot intentionally allocate additional study time to correct it.

Retrieval practice can improve both memory and calibration when learners compare an attempted answer with corrective feedback. The benefit is not simply that testing strengthens memory. Retrieval creates observable evidence about what the learner can and cannot currently produce without assistance. When feedback reveals a mismatch between confidence and actual performance, subsequent control decisions can become better calibrated. However, repeated testing without informative feedback may strengthen incorrect responses or leave the learner unaware of why an answer failed.

Effective self-regulated learning therefore depends on a loop rather than a single judgment: perform, monitor, compare, and adjust. Local regulation may involve deciding whether to repeat the current problem, whereas global regulation concerns larger decisions such as changing study methods or reallocating time across topics. Neither form of control guarantees learning. Their usefulness depends partly on whether the monitoring signal accurately represents the learner’s current state and whether the chosen control action is appropriate for the underlying problem.
